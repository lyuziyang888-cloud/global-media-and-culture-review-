# Unit 1 Reading: Canada's Showbiz Steps Out of Hollywood's Shadow

## Source

Stevis-Gridneff, M. (2026, June 13). *Canada's Showbiz Steps Out of Hollywood's Shadow*. *The New York Times*.

## Central Argument

Canada's screen industry shows that global reach and local cultural identity can coexist, but this depends on production, representation, public funding and regulation rather than filming location alone.

## Concepts and Evidence

| Concept | Exam-ready evidence | What it demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| local content | *Heated Rivalry* has Canadian creators, crew, most cast, locations and imagery | media nationality is constructed through several production and textual features |
| cultural identity | the article contrasts explicitly Canadian storytelling with generically North American production | global circulation does not automatically erase national distinction |
| representation | *North of North* is largely created, acted and produced by Indigenous Canadians | authorship and production participation shape representation |
| streaming platforms | HBO carried *Heated Rivalry* in the US; Netflix distributes *North of North* globally | foreign platforms can expand local content's reach |
| media regulation | the CRTC proposed a 15% levy on major streamers' Canadian revenue for domestic and Indigenous content, but government blocked it | states contest how global platforms contribute to local production |
| public funding | Canadian taxpayers partly subsidised *Heated Rivalry* | national media capacity is supported by cultural policy, not only markets |

## Fast Exam Use

> *Heated Rivalry* demonstrates that a program can circulate globally while remaining locally identifiable because its creators, labour, locations and imagery are strongly Canadian.

> *North of North* shows how a global platform can support Indigenous visibility, although distribution power remains partly controlled by Netflix.
