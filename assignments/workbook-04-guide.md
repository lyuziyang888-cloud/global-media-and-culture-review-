## Prescribed Reading Map

### Yoon: Cultural Hybridity

Yoon defines hybridity as both cultural fusion and fragmentation. Distinct forms and identities mix, while fixed ideas of culture are also challenged (2018, p. 375). K-pop demonstrates this through its mixture of Western pop conventions, Korean production and multilingual or multinational elements.

#### Imagination of Participatory Globalisation

Overseas fans treat K-pop as a participatory space. They translate, interpret, imitate and re-appropriate music, videos, performances and idols. K-pop's hybrid elements reduce cultural and linguistic barriers, while reaction videos, parodies and cover dance groups demonstrate interaction with original texts (Yoon, 2018, pp. 377-380).

**Use for comments that:**

- praise performers for recreating or adapting choreography
- compare the cover with the original
- express a desire to learn, perform or join the practice
- show fans interpreting the performance together

**Critical limitation:** Participation may also become unpaid fan labour that benefits platforms and entertainment industries (Yoon, 2018, p. 380).

#### Imagination of Cosmopolitan Globalisation

Fans imagine a more inclusive global cultural space. They use K-pop to question Western dominance and reject claims that Korean fans exclusively own K-pop. Fans reinterpret K-pop through their own experiences and locations rather than treating it as culturally fixed (Yoon, 2018, pp. 380-382).

**Use for comments that:**

- identify the performers' country or local setting positively
- celebrate people from different countries sharing K-pop
- welcome performers regardless of nationality or language
- challenge the idea that only Korean performers or fans can participate authentically

### Jin: Transcultural Fandom

Jin distinguishes transcultural fandom from a framework focused mainly on movement between nations. A transcultural approach examines a wider combination of cultural context, gender, ethnicity, age and fan culture. Fans from different backgrounds form communities around shared interests and **affective affinity**, meaning a subjective feeling of connection to a fan object regardless of its national origin (2024, pp. 119-121).

**Application to cover dance:** A cover can be transcultural when performers take a Korean-produced fan object into their own location and identity, while viewers connect through shared enthusiasm rather than nationality alone. The argument becomes stronger when the comments show recognition, belonging or interaction among people from different backgrounds.

**Critical limitation:** Jin states that transculturalism does not fully explain why every global fan likes K-pop. Industry control, platform visibility and national context still matter (2024, p. 133).

## Selected Case: BLACKPINK - How You Like That

### Official Music Video

- **Video:** [BLACKPINK - How You Like That M/V](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ioNng23DkIM)
- **Uploader:** BLACKPINK Official Artist Channel
- **Release date:** 26 June 2020
- **Agency:** YG Entertainment
- **Why it qualifies:** the official artist channel uploaded the original MV

### Cover Dance Video

- **Video:** [KPOP IN PUBLIC PARIS - How You Like That Dance Cover](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJfuX3ZBIPE)
- **Uploader:** RISIN'
- **Release date:** 10 July 2020
- **Location:** Paris, France
- **Performers:** four male dancers in the principal BLACKPINK roles, supported by additional dancers
- **Why it works:** it keeps the recognisable choreography while changing the performers, costumes, production setting and cultural location

### Prompt 1 Comparison Plan

| Compare | Official MV | Paris cover | Analytical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Choreography | BLACKPINK performs the agency-produced choreography | RISIN' retains the principal movements and formations | shows continuity and fan reproduction |
| Performers | four female K-pop idols | four male dancers from a Paris-based cover group | shows that gender does not prevent affective affinity or participation |
| Location | controlled studio sets and rapidly changing visual worlds | several recognisable public locations in Paris | relocates a Korean media text into the dancers' local environment |
| Costume | high-budget styling, including modernised hanbok | fan-selected outfits inspired by the original style | demonstrates adaptation rather than exact duplication |
| Production | cinematic editing, sets and effects | public performance with simpler fan production | shifts attention from spectacle to choreography and participation |

### Verified Video Moments

#### Similarity: Recognisable Choreography

- **Official MV, 0:58-1:03:** BLACKPINK performs the pre-chorus sequence as a four-member formation in the garden set, including the hand movements around the face and upper body.
- **Paris cover, 0:42-0:47:** the principal dancers reproduce the corresponding sequence and formation after the cover's shorter lead-in.
- **Use in analysis:** the retained movement makes the cover recognisable as *How You Like That* and demonstrates fan reproduction of the source choreography.

#### Difference: How the Final Group Sequence Is Presented

- **Official MV, 2:42-2:50:** the final group choreography is presented through a constructed indoor set, coordinated background dancers, close framing and rapid cuts.
- **Paris cover, 2:51-2:59:** the corresponding group section is performed in an open Paris public space, with the dancers' full bodies and formation remaining visible in wider shots.
- **Use in analysis:** the cover preserves the choreography while changing its performers, location and mode of presentation. This relocation gives the Korean-produced routine a local French performance context.

### Best Initial Argument

The Paris cover preserves BLACKPINK's choreography but relocates it through different performers, clothing and public space. This makes the video a useful example of participatory globalisation and transcultural fandom because fans reproduce the K-pop text through their own identities and location.

## Prompt 1: Video Comparison

Do not list every visual difference. Select evidence that helps explain how fans reproduce and transform K-pop.

Use this pattern:

```text
similarity + timestamp
-> difference + timestamp
-> explain what the change shows about fan participation or adaptation
```

Possible comparison points include choreography, formation, costume, location, performer identity, camera movement, editing, production quality and interaction with public space.

## Prompt 2: Three-Comment Analysis

Analyse each comment separately:

```text
identify the comment
-> name Yoon's relevant form of hybridity
-> explain the comment's language or meaning
-> connect it to the fan's experience of K-pop across cultures
```

The task suggests that comments may reflect:

- **imagination of participatory culture**
- **imagination of cosmopolitan globalisation**

Use the meanings above and cite the relevant pages. A comment such as “great cover” is usually too weak unless its wording clearly reveals participation, identity, cultural connection or cross-border imagination.

## Prompt 3: Transcultural Fandom

State a clear position on whether cover dance is a transcultural fandom practice. Apply Jin's definition rather than treating “international” and “transcultural” as synonyms.

A strong evaluation should consider:

- fans reproduce a Korean cultural product outside its original production context
- performers may adapt the choreography through their own bodies, location and style
- comments can create interaction among audiences across national and linguistic boundaries
- participation may still depend on entertainment-company choreography, platform visibility and established K-pop conventions

The final judgement should explain both fan agency and its limits.
