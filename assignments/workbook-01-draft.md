## Global Streaming Investment Beyond the United States

Kupczyk (2025) identifies a lasting shift in scripted television commissioning by Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Apple TV+, Paramount+ and Max. North America's share of these streamers' first-run and renewed series orders fell from 45.9% in the first half of 2023 to 37% in the second half of 2024. Although this trend supports cultural globalisation by demonstrating more geographically diverse media flows, media imperialism provides the stronger explanation because a small group of predominantly US-based corporations still controls where, why and how this investment occurs.

From a media-imperialism perspective, the report demonstrates continuing concentration of ownership and unequal structural power. The decline in North American production does not mean that US corporate dominance has disappeared: the commissioners remain powerful transnational streaming companies headquartered in the United States. Their control over capital and distribution allows them to select which local industries gain global visibility. This creates dependency because producers in other regions may receive investment while remaining reliant on foreign platforms for financing and access to international audiences (Mirrlees, 2013, p. XX). Ampere's finding that streamers prioritise Asia-Pacific originals to attract subscribers further suggests that cultural investment follows corporate expansion rather than an equal redistribution of media power. Moreover, Central and South America, Central and Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa retain only modest shares of commissions, revealing persistent centre-periphery inequalities.

Cultural globalisation nevertheless explains important changes that a simple one-way model overlooks. First, media flows are becoming more multidirectional. Western Europe's share of commissions increased from 22.5% in early 2022 to 31.9% in early 2024, briefly exceeding North America's 30.3%. Asia Pacific also rose from 17.4% to 26.2% over the same period, supported by investment in Japan, India and Thailand. These patterns challenge the idea that global culture moves only from the United States to passive peripheral markets (Mirrlees, 2013, p. XX). Second, the strategy illustrates interaction between global platforms and local culture. Streamers commission local originals to meet culturally specific audience demands, while some of these productions subsequently become popular in other markets. This global-local interaction can increase cultural diversity and create hybrid media that is simultaneously locally grounded and transnationally circulated.

Overall, cultural globalisation better captures the diversification of production locations and the growing international circulation of non-US stories. However, media imperialism more convincingly explains the power structure producing that diversity. The report's changes are strategic decisions by a concentrated group of global streamers responding to production costs, slowing North American subscriber growth and commercial opportunities abroad. Therefore, the geography of content is becoming less US-centred, but control over global commissioning remains highly concentrated.
