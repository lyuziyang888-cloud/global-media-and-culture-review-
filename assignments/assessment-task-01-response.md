# Global Streaming Investment Beyond the United States

Kupczyk (2025) identifies a lasting shift in scripted television commissioning by Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Apple TV+, Paramount+ and Max. North America's share of these streamers' first-run and renewed series orders fell from 45.9% in the first half of 2023 to 37% in the second half of 2024. Although this trend demonstrates increasingly diverse global media flows, media imperialism provides the stronger explanation because US-based corporations continue to control where, why and how investment occurs.

A media-imperialism scholar would first emphasise foreign corporate control over media production and distribution (Mirrlees, 2013, p. 23). The decline in North American production does not mean that US media power has disappeared: the companies commissioning, financing and distributing this content remain powerful US-based transnational corporations. Their control allows them to select which local industries and genres receive global visibility. Second, the report demonstrates a dominant US media centre, weaker peripheries and dependency (Mirrlees, 2013, pp. 24-25). Asia-Pacific producers may gain investment and international access while remaining reliant on foreign platforms for finance and distribution. The modest shares retained by Central and South America, Central and Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa further show that global opportunities remain uneven. Investment is directed towards markets that serve platform growth rather than being equally redistributed among local industries.

A cultural-globalisation scholar would instead emphasise the rise of non-US regional media centres and multidirectional or contra-flows (Mirrlees, 2013, p. 42). Western Europe's share of commissions increased from 22.5% in early 2022 to 31.9% in early 2024, briefly exceeding North America's 30.3%. Asia Pacific also rose from 17.4% to 26.2% over the same period. These changes demonstrate greater production capacity outside the US and challenge a one-way model in which media moves only from the United States to passive peripheral markets. Streamers' investment in Japan, India and Thailand may place more non-US stories within transnational circulation. However, as Mirrlees cautions, multidirectional flows do not by themselves demonstrate the disappearance of US dominance.

Cultural globalisation explains the diversification of production locations and the growing visibility of non-US content. However, its strongest form risks treating geographical diversity as evidence that power has also been redistributed. Mirrlees's continuity-and-change approach retains cultural globalisation's insight into new complexity while preserving cultural imperialism's attention to unequal structures (2013, pp. 49-50). Media imperialism therefore better explains the power structure producing this diversity. The shift is organised by a concentrated group of US streaming corporations responding to production costs, subscriber growth and commercial opportunities abroad. Scripted production is becoming less US-centred, but control over global commissioning and distribution remains highly concentrated.
