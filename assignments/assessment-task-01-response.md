# Global Streaming Investment Beyond the United States

Kupczyk (2025) reports a lasting geographical shift in scripted television commissioning by Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Apple TV+, Paramount+ and Max. North America's share of these streamers' first-run and renewed series orders fell from 45.9% in the first half of 2023 to 37% in the second half of 2024. High production costs, slowing domestic subscriber growth and disruption from the 2023 writers' and actors' strikes contributed to this decline, while platforms expanded investment in international growth markets. Although this trend reflects a less US-centred production landscape, media imperialism explains it more convincingly because corporate control has not decentralised alongside production.

A media-imperialism scholar would first emphasise foreign corporate control. Mirrlees defines media imperialism through external pressure over media ownership, structure, distribution or content without proportionate reciprocal influence (2013, p. 23). Here, predominantly US-based platforms still decide where commissions go and control the distribution systems through which programmes reach audiences. Second, the pattern retains a centre-periphery hierarchy and dependency (Mirrlees, 2013, pp. 24-25). Producers in Japan, India and Thailand may receive investment and international access, but their opportunities remain shaped by foreign platforms' subscriber strategies. Meanwhile, Central and South America, Central and Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa retain only modest commissioning shares. The expansion is therefore selective rather than an equal redistribution of media capacity.

A cultural-globalisation scholar would focus instead on the rise of regional media centres and increasingly multidirectional flows (Mirrlees, 2013, p. 42). Western Europe's commissioning share rose from 22.5% in early 2022 to 31.9% in early 2024, briefly surpassing North America's 30.3%. Asia Pacific also rose from 17.4% to 26.2%, demonstrating stronger production capacity outside the traditional US centre. Investment in local originals, combined with the growing popularity of some Asia-Pacific programmes in other markets, challenges a purely one-way model of US cultural export. Nevertheless, Mirrlees cautions that multidirectional and contra-flows can coexist with continuing US dominance.

Cultural globalisation therefore explains the geographical diversification of production, but it risks mistaking diversity for redistributed power. Moreover, the report measures commissions rather than content ownership, intellectual-property rights, revenue distribution or local producers' decision-making authority. It demonstrates decentralised production more clearly than decentralised control. Mirrlees's continuity-and-change approach recognises new global complexity while retaining cultural imperialism's attention to unequal structures (2013, pp. 49-50). Media imperialism consequently offers the stronger explanation: non-US industries are producing more content, yet a concentrated group of US corporations continues to organise investment and global distribution.
