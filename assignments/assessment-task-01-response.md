# Global Streaming Investment Beyond the United States

Kupczyk (2025) identifies a lasting shift in scripted television commissioning by Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Apple TV+, Paramount+ and Max. North America's share of these streamers' first-run and renewed series orders fell from 45.9% in the first half of 2023 to 37% in the second half of 2024. Although this trend demonstrates increasingly diverse global media flows, media imperialism provides the stronger explanation because US-based corporations continue to control where, why and how investment occurs.

A media-imperialism scholar would first emphasise concentrated corporate ownership and control. The decline in North American production does not mean that US media power has disappeared: the companies commissioning, financing and distributing this content remain powerful US-based transnational corporations. Their control allows them to select which local industries and genres receive global visibility. Second, the report demonstrates unequal core-periphery relations and dependency (Mirrlees, 2013). Asia-Pacific producers may gain investment and international access while remaining reliant on foreign platforms for finance and distribution. The fact that Central and South America, Central and Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa retain only modest shares of commissions further shows that global opportunities remain uneven. Investment is directed towards markets that serve platform growth rather than being equally redistributed among local industries.

A cultural-globalisation scholar would instead interpret the data as evidence of multidirectional media flows. Western Europe's share of commissions increased from 22.5% in early 2022 to 31.9% in early 2024, briefly exceeding North America's 30.3%. Asia Pacific also rose from 17.4% to 26.2% over the same period. These changes challenge a one-way model in which media flows only from the United States to passive peripheral markets (Mirrlees, 2013). The strategy also demonstrates global-local interaction and increased cultural diversity. Streamers commission local originals in Japan, India and Thailand to respond to regional audience demand, while some of this content becomes popular in other markets. Local cultures are therefore not simply replaced by a uniform American culture; they increasingly contribute stories to transnational circulation.

Cultural globalisation explains the diversification of production locations and the growing visibility of non-US content. It also corrects media imperialism's tendency to understate the agency of non-US producers and the ability of local content to cross borders. However, cultural globalisation risks treating geographical diversity as evidence that power has also been redistributed. Media imperialism better explains the power structure producing this diversity. The shift is organised by a concentrated group of US streaming corporations responding to production costs, slowing North American subscriber growth and commercial opportunities abroad. Therefore, scripted production is becoming less US-centred, but control over global commissioning and distribution remains highly concentrated.
