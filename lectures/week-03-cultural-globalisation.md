# Week 3 Lecture: Cultural Globalisation

## Central Purpose

The lecture introduces cultural globalisation as a group of theories that corrects the limits of media imperialism by examining audiences, workers, identities, meaning-making and multidirectional media movement. It concludes that both paradigms are useful and should be combined rather than treated as absolute opposites.

## Lecture Structure

1. recap the limitations of media imperialism
2. compare macro structural and micro cultural approaches
3. introduce cultural globalisation as a paradigm
4. explain the five-scapes model
5. examine asymmetrical interdependence and cultural proximity
6. explain contra-flows and media capitals
7. evaluate criticisms of cultural globalisation
8. compare what both paradigms contribute

## Key Concepts and Definitions

- **Cultural globalisation:** a group of theories and concepts explaining how people, media and cultures interact across borders through contextual, multidirectional and unequal processes.
- **Five-scapes model:** Appadurai's account of globalisation through overlapping and deterritorialised financescapes, ethnoscapes, ideoscapes, technoscapes and mediascapes.
- **Asymmetrical interdependence:** countries, industries and cultures are connected, but their resources and influence remain unequal.
- **Cultural proximity:** audiences often prefer media that feels close to their language, humour, religion, values, lifestyle or social experience.
- **Contra-flow:** media movement from former peripheries or the Global South towards other peripheral and dominant markets.
- **Media capital:** a dynamic centre with concentrated resources, reputation, talent and the capacity for efficient, sustained media production.

## Main Arguments

- Media imperialism can overstate US power, treat national cultures as uniform and struggle to explain active audiences or successful non-US media.
- Cultural globalisation moves from a macro-level "helicopter view" towards ground-level meaning-making and cultural identification.
- Global processes are contextual rather than uniform; the five scapes overlap differently across places.
- Audience preference often follows cultural proximity, but socioeconomic status, broadband access, habits and proximity to the US can modify it.
- Multidirectional and contra-flows challenge a simple US-to-rest model without eliminating unequal one-way flows.
- Media capitals are relational and historically contingent, not merely equivalent to nation-states.
- Cultural globalisation can become too abstract or celebratory and overlook ownership and power.
- A strong global-media analysis uses media imperialism for structures and continuities, and cultural globalisation for people, meanings and change.

## Cases and Evidence

- **Brazilian television in the 1980s-90s:** imported content declined, while education level shaped preferences for US and culturally proximate programming.
- **Latin American audiences, 2004-14:** domestic preference remained important, but socioeconomic and technological factors complicated a simple proximity rule.
- **Jane the Virgin:** Venezuela's *Juana la virgen* was adapted in Poland, the US, Turkey, Greece, Israel, South Korea, Mexico and India, illustrating contra-flow and adaptation.
- **Hollywood and other media capitals:** concentrations of resources, reputation and talent can produce sustained output and attract creative workers.

## Assessment and Exam Signals

Be able to define each paradigm, identify its characteristics and limitations, and explain which examples each handles best. Mirrlees's Chapter 1 and cultural materialism support a middle-ground approach: analyse both unequal structures and active cultural meaning-making.

Workbook 1 requires two ideas from each paradigm and a judgement about which better explains the selected report.
